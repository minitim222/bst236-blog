PrismRestore: A Compact Restoration Toolbox for Agentic Image Restoration

Zhaocheng Yu, Kui Jiang, Jing Cao, Yiang Chen, Xiaocheng Feng, Junjun Jiang, Xianming Liu • 2026-11-18

Agentic image restoration composes operations to handle mixed degradations, yet its toolbox often requires storing multiple restoration networks. We propose PrismRestore, which constructs a compact bank of callable restoration actions from a single frozen all-in-one model. Our approach builds on task-dependent yet overlapping channel responses in the shared model. An offline task-prior search uses activation and gradient statistics to group channels with similar response profiles, then searches task-specific modulation coefficients over non-representative channels while leaving representative channels unmodulated. The resulting prior bank enables explicit action selection and composition without additional restoration backbones or task-specific adapter modules. A state-conditioned policy learns to compose these actions using Group Relative Policy Optimization. Its objective separately normalizes fidelity and perceptual feedback within groups of trajectories sampled for the same input, and combines terminal evaluation with return-to-go derived from stepwise quality changes. Experiments on synthetic composite-degradation and real-world multi-task benchmarks show improvements over the evaluated all-in-one baselines and restoration quality competitive with multi-model agents. PrismRestore achieves these results with a single restoration backbone and faster inference than the evaluated agentic baselines.

New Observational Insights into Generalised Axion-Like Dark Energy

Mariam Bouhmadi-López, Hsu-Wen Chiang, Carlos G. Boiza, Thomas J. Broadhust • 2026-11-18

We consider a quintessence model based on a generalised axion-like scalar field potential, originally introduced as a phenomenological framework to describe the late-time accelerated expansion of the Universe. The model exhibits a tracking regime that alleviates the coincidence problem and naturally leads to a phase of late-time acceleration driven by an effective cosmological constant, without requiring fine-tuning of the scalar field initial conditions. In this work, we confront the background cosmological evolution of this model with current observations by performing a parameter inference analysis using cosmic microwave background distance priors, baryon acoustic oscillation measurements, Type Ia supernovae, the SH0ES distance ladder, cosmic chronometers, and gamma-ray bursts. This analysis allows us to assess the observational viability of the model, its ability to reconcile different cosmological datasets, and its statistical performance relative to $Λ$CDM.

When Time-Reversible Sensor Records Require Hidden Dissipation

Andrey V. Dobrynin, Anastasia Stroujkova • 2026-11-18

A binary sensor record can be time reversible yet require dissipation in every compatible finite-state Markov model. For a four-state sensor with correlated dwell times, two observed switching statistics give an exact equilibrium criterion and, when equilibrium is excluded, a positive lower bound on entropy production independent of hidden-state number. An explicit four-state construction provides an upper bound on the minimum dissipation. For a noisy Poisson sensor, the bounds differ by less than a factor of two and vanish at a kinetic matching point while the sensor remains dissipative.

Efficient search for microlensed gravitational-wave signals with matched filtering and a two-image statistic

Soumen Roy, Justin Janquart • 2026-11-18

Gravitational waves from compact binary coalescences can be microlensed by compact objects along the line of sight, producing frequency-dependent waveform distortions. As standard matched-filter searches rely on unlensed templates without accounting for lensing effects, microlensed signals can lose signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) through template mismatch and suffer further losses from signal-consistency tests. We quantify both effects for the $\texttt{PyCBC}$ search using microlensed injections in simulated data. The two losses occupy different regions of lens parameter space in the geometric optics representation: template mismatch is governed by the relative magnification of the two images, while the consistency-test penalty is governed by their time delay. We show that a microlensed signal lies in the two-dimensional subspace spanned by a template and its time-shifted copy, allowing the relative magnification and Morse phase to be maximized over analytically, leaving only the time delay to search. The resulting statistic requires no extension of the template bank and reduces the median loss in re-weighted SNR from $10\%$ to $1.6\%$, and the $90^\mathrm{th}$ percentile from $41\%$ to $9\%$. At a false-alarm rate of one per day, the relative detection volume improves by about $6\%$ across all injections, and by a factor of $1.9$ for the $10\%$ of injections most affected by lensing. In addition to increased detection capacities, we also show how the detection statistic can be used to establish the lensed nature of the trigger. Therefore, the work proposed here lays the foundation for an effective search and identification of microlensed signals in gravitational-wave data.

Statistical methods for extrapolation from one population to another: a review

Lingjiao Wang, Francois Mercier, Astrid Jullion, Xuan Zhu, Christian Rover, Marc Vandemeulebroecke, Martin Posch, Sarah Zohar, Nigel Stallard • 2026-11-18

Background: Extrapolation refers to statistical approaches that support inference on treatment effects in a target population using data from completed clinical trials conducted in a source population. This is particularly important when only limited data are available in the target population. Extrapolation may be based on multiple sources of information, including pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, mechanistic, or clinical outcome data. This review focuses on methods that enable borrowing of information from one population to support inference in another. Methods: We conducted a review of statistical methodologies for extrapolation, focusing on approaches that incorporate outcome data from source populations to support inference in target populations. Methods were identified across a range of settings, including extrapolation across age groups and across different clinical indications. Literature searches were conducted using the Science Citation Index Expanded database within Web of Science and included all English-language records available up to 30 June 2025. Results: The review identified 218 extrapolation approaches from 159 papers, which can be broadly classified into three main categories: Bayesian, frequentist, and causal inference methods. These methods vary in their assumptions, implementation, and applicability across different extrapolation settings. Conclusions: This review provides a comprehensive and up-to-date overview of statistical methods for extrapolation, offering a foundation for further methodological development and applied research. It also discusses key considerations for method selection and current limitations, and highlights priorities for future research, including the development of new extrapolation methods and clinical trial designs that facilitate extrapolation.

Can Programs Replace LLM Judges? Executable Program Judges for Scalable and Efficient Relevance Labeling

Eugene Agichtein, Charles L. A. Clarke, Kaustubh Dhole, William Stern • 2026-11-18

Large language model (LLM) judges have become a common way to create large-scale relevance judgments for retrieval evaluation. However, each judgment requires a potentially expensive call to a frontier model, and the judgments are hard to interpret and may not be reproducible. In this paper, we ask whether an LLM can write a readable program for each topic that replaces its per-document judgments. To address these issues, we propose ExecJudges, a method for generating fast, inspectable and scalable judges for automatic relevance labeling. Specifically, ExecJudges uses a frontier LLM to turn assessment guidelines into executable programs, which rely only on lexical matching, standard NLP libraries like parsers, and pool statistics. An ExecJudges-L variant also uses examples of human labels if available. We evaluate ExecJudges on seven TREC collections by comparing the produced labels with trained NIST assessors, frontier LLM judges, and recently proposed per-topic classifiers. Our results show that ExecJudges agrees with NIST assessors at the level of the Claude Sonnet 5.5 LLM judges on TREC DL 2019-2021. With a small number of human labels per topic, the ExecJudges-L variant agrees with NIST more than per-topic classifiers with the same labels on all seven collections, and either exceeds the agreement of LLM judges provided the same labels or is within about 0.05 of it in Krippendorff's alpha. The labels rank the official TREC DL runs with Kendall's tau of 0.87 to 0.94, and writing the programs incurs a one-time cost of $0.17 to $0.49 per topic, with no additional costs for grading. Our results suggest that ExecJudges offers a reproducible, inspectable, efficient and cost-effective alternative to LLM judges for labeling deep result pools for system evaluation or ranking improvements.

Shedding Light on Quantum Non-Classicality: Witnessing Wigner Negativity with Free Electrons

Sunjun Hwang, Leong Chuan Kwek • 2026-11-18

Certifying quantum non-classicality like Wigner negativity typically demands full optical state tomography. This work introduces a noise-resilient framework for witnessing optical Wigner negativity using phase-resolved quantum photon-induced near-field electron microscopy (QPINEM). By mapping electron coherences directly to symmetrically ordered optical characteristic functions within a coupling-limited phase-space region, the protocol bypasses full optical reconstruction. A post-measurement witness matrix is constructed via Bochner's theorem from recorded electron spectra, where negative eigenvalues certify non-classicality. To ensure reliability under realistic experimental conditions, a noise-aware selection procedure decouples discovery from statistical validation. Benchmarked across various optical preparations-including vacuum-Fock superpositions, odd cat states, and GKP states-the protocol provides a statistically bounded pathway for probing quantum features with free electrons.

Overlooked and Underutilized: Prototypes are the Missing Piece in Tabular XAI

Jacek Karolczak, Jerzy Stefanowski • 2026-11-18

Explainable AI (XAI) is frequently applied to high-stakes tabular data, yet the field relies heavily on feature attribution methods. While these approaches offer technical transparency, evaluating dozens of isolated features demands excessive cognitive effort from non-expert users. In this paper, we argue that prototypes remain overlooked and underutilized, and should be integrated as a complementary, intuitive gateway to auditing complex machine learning models. Although entire exemplars function as an intuitive baseline for prototype-based explanations, we highlight prototypical parts as particularly valuable for tabular data. By grouping related features into bounded subspaces, prototypical parts technically embody human-understandable concepts, replacing isolated features with cohesive semantic units. We support this integration through two primary lenses. Cognitively, these parts align with Rosch's categorization theory and Kahneman's System 1 thinking to naturally reduce cognitive load for humans. Then, we introduce a Geometric Validity Triad to conceptualize why prototypes validate the data manifold - a critical dimension often neglected by existing methods. We contend that making tabular XAI genuinely comprehensible requires shifting toward composite explanation pipelines that pair intuitive similarity-based reasoning with rigorous feature-level auditing. The paper concludes by outlining open challenges of prototype-based methods and discussing potential solutions to guide future research.

A Foundation Model to Measure and Predict Human Behaviour

Haoran Ye, Tianrui Huo, Hangzhen Li, Xin Zhang, Guojie Song • 2026-11-18

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to infer psychological attributes, predict individual behaviour, and simulate human populations. However, despite generating plausible responses, they often misrepresent individuals and distort population statistics. Here we introduce Chiron, a foundation model to measure and predict human behaviour. We jointly fine-tune an LLM to represent psychological constructs, predict individual behaviour, and align population response distributions with human statistics. The training and evaluation data span 36 behavioural domains and 78 social science experiments, comprising 288,307 person records, 1,411,750 atomic behavioural decisions, and 99,953 psychometric assessments over 109 psychological constructs. In our evaluations, Chiron's psychological measurements agree more closely with human self-reports and exhibit fewer biases that undermine psychometric validity. It predicts individual behaviour more accurately than frontier LLMs (up to 28.4% relative improvement). In simulations of social science experiments, it more accurately captures the direction and magnitude of human experimental effects (82.2--94.3% lower effect-size estimation error). Together, these results establish Chiron as a unified computational instrument for measuring and predicting human behaviour.

On the Validity of Fisher Randomization Tests under the Weak Null

Timothy Sudijono, Edgar Dobriban, Eric Tchetgen Tchetgen • 2026-11-18

Consider the problem of testing the Neyman weak null of no sample average treatment effect $H_{0N}:τ= 0$ in randomized experiments with binary outcomes. Although the Fisher Randomization Test (FRT) is typically justified under Fisher's sharp null of no individual treatment effect, we show that it is finite-sample-valid for the weak null $H_{0N}$ in three common combinations of study design and test statistic: 1) balanced completely randomized experiments with the difference-in-means test statistic, 2) matched pairs experiments with the same test statistic, and 3) balanced Bernoulli randomized experiments with the centered Horvitz-Thompson test statistic. As an immediate corollary, Fisher's exact test in a balanced completely randomized experiment is valid under the weak null, and so is McNemar's test in a matched pairs experiment. Similar results hold for the one-sided null $H_0:τ\leq 0$. Beyond balanced designs, we show that twice the one-sided FRT $p$-value with the same test statistics is valid under $H_{0N}$. In simulation experiments, the FRTs compare favorably to other exact procedures.

A Homogeneous Benchmark of Exoplanet Mass-Radius Relations

Ailar Alizadehsabegh, Soroush Sarabi, Atila Poro • 2026-11-18

The mass-radius ($M$-$R$) relation is fundamental to inferring the bulk composition and internal structure of exoplanets. However, published relations remain difficult to compare directly because studies adopt different planetary samples, fitting procedures, and evaluation metrics. We construct a homogeneous, quality-controlled benchmark of 963 exoplanets with dynamically measured masses and transit radii from the NASA Exoplanet Archive. We compare empirical power laws, probabilistic models, and machine-learning (ML) regressors using point accuracy, interval coverage, and probabilistic calibration. On the full test set, a multi-feature random forest has the lowest root-mean-square error, 0.329~dex in $\log_{10} M$ versus 0.368~dex for a fitted two-parameter power law ($p = 0.007$ unadjusted; $p = 0.059$ after Holm correction across 13 comparisons). Radius-only ML models perform comparably to the single power law. In three ranges of the measured radius, several methods improve on the power law below 1.6 $R_\oplus$, but none does between 1.6 and 4 $R_\oplus$. Including stellar and orbital covariates significantly improves predictions only for low-mass planets ($M < 4.4\,M_\oplus$), although this result is sensitive to missing covariate data and weakens when the training and test sets are separated by host star. Within their calibrated radius domains, the published empirical and probabilistic relations remain highly competitive. Finally, a first-order variance decomposition shows that formal measurement uncertainties account for only $\sim$3\% of the residual variance (0.067 of 0.401~dex), and the remaining $\sim$0.396~dex arises from compositional diversity, selection effects, and model approximation error.

MLIR-MRacle: Memory-Model-Aware Metamorphic Fuzzing of Concurrent MLIR

Aaron Gabayoyo, Aidan Dakhama, Ajitha Rajan • 2026-11-18

Multi-level compilers built on MLIR lower concurrent programs through many stages towards targets with potentially drastically different memory models, where an optimisation which appears reasonable, can silently change program behaviour. Testing these lowerings is hard: a concurrent program often has no single expected outcome, and existing MLIR fuzzers often assume determinism. We present MLIR-MRacle, a memory-model-aware metamorphic fuzzer for concurrent MLIR programs. MLIR-MRacle applies metamorphic relations gated by the target memory model, so that both the original and transformed programs remain well defined - executing both repeatedly, and comparing their outcome sets or distributions withone of two statistical oracles. A coverage guided campaign driver built on libafl is used to perform continuous fuzzing. MRs, memory models, oracles, and execution backends all provide extension points for future researchers and compiler engineers to build off this package. In a case study on Armv8 hardware, MLIR-MRacle ran over 145k trials across four configurations, reaching up to 93.6k compiler coverage edges, and found a bug in the OpenMP dialect verifier that has since been fixed upstream. Cross-Dataset Inference of Central-Satellite Classification and Host Halo Masses from Galaxy neighbours with Machine Learning

Alice Chen, Camille Avestruz, Niayesh Afshordi, Yen-Ting Lin • 2026-11-18

The classification of galaxies as centrals or satellites gives insight into the galaxy-halo connection. This, together with halo mass estimates, can be used to connect observed galaxy populations to underlying dark matter structure. To (1) classify galaxies as centrals or satellites and (2) estimate the host halo mass for centrals, we train XGBoost models on IllustrisTNG (TNG100) galaxies, using the distances (3D or projected (2+1)D redshift space) and $M_*$ of their ten nearest neighbours. We then test the model on EAGLE to measure its robustness after applying a simple domain-alignment step that corrects for a relative shift in the $M_*$ distribution between TNG100 and EAGLE. We further augment the training data by combining feature sets from neighbour samples with varying lower $M_*$ thresholds. On the TNG100 test set, the central/satellite classifier reaches purity and completeness $=0.97$ (0.95) in 3D (projected redshift) space at the threshold where purity=completeness, and the halo-mass regressor achieves $r^2$=0.98 (0.93) when evaluated on true centrals and $r^2=0.93$ (0.93) when evaluated on inferred centrals. Applied to EAGLE after alignment, the classifier achieves purity/completeness $=0.95$ (0.95), while halo-mass inference yields $r^2=0.91$ ($0.87$) for true centrals and $r^2=0.83$ (0.77) for inferred centrals; training-data augmentation most improves the halo-mass estimation task. Satellites misclassified as centrals underpredict the true masses of host halos, especially if they are closer to a lower mass halo's central than its own host's central and/or has a significantly lower $M_*$ than its own central. These results show that survey-accessible information such as projected separations and stellar masses - combined with simulation-trained models and domain alignment - can enable reliable central identification and host halo mass estimation to an $r^2\geq 0.85$.

Soft Mixture of Quantum Experts for Hybrid Quantum-Classical Learning

Valeria Repetto, Romina D'Aurizio, Piero Farinelli • 2026-11-18

Quantum Machine Learning (QML) has attracted increasing interest as a possible route to construct feature representations that are difficult to reproduce with classical models. However, near-term QML architectures based on trainable parametrized quantum circuits remain affected by limited hardware resources, noise, and severe optimization difficulties such as barren plateaus. In this work we propose Soft-MoQE, a Soft Mixture of Quantum Experts architecture that replaces variational quantum training with the input-dependent selection and combination of fixed quantum feature maps. The model is composed of a heterogeneous pool of non-trainable quantum circuits, each defining a distinct embedding, and a classical gating network that learns how to weight these embeddings for each data point. We benchmark Soft-MoQE on three PCA-reduced tasks: MNIST parity, MNIST 10-class, and FashionMNIST. Soft-MoQE outperforms the single best expert, the uniform average of all experts and a linear baseline, achieving task-dependent performance comparable to that of a trainable quantum neural network, without optimizing any quantum parameter and with a quantum training cost that is lower by orders of magnitude. The analysis of the class- conditioned expert usage shows that the learned routing combines a few experts for each input, with a selection that depends on the class. These results support the learned routing among fixed quantum circuits as a trainability-aware design principle for hybrid QML.

Exploring Speaker-Aware Engagement Modeling for Children with Autism Spectrum Disorder

Kaleen Shrestha, Austin Joel Dympep, Young Suh Hong, Maja Matarić • 2026-11-18

Prior work on engagement modeling in children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) has demonstrated substantial individual variability in behavioral features, particularly in visual modalities. In this work, we extend the literature to acoustic features in naturalistic interactions, where multiple speakers and atypical interaction patterns complicate speaker-specific analysis. We investigated whether speaker diarization improved acoustic engagement modeling during month-long, in-home socially assistive robot (SAR) interventions with seven children with ASD. We trained classical machine learning models using acoustic features extracted from diarized child and parent/caregiver audio and compared their performance with models trained on undiarized audio. Diarized audio consistently yielded higher area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) values, although these differences were not statistically significant. More importantly, the relative contributions of child and caregiver acoustic features varied across participants, indicating that the acoustic information associated with engagement was not consistent across children. These findings extend evidence of participant-level variability in ASD engagement modeling to the acoustic domain and demonstrate the feasibility of isolating speaker-specific vocalizations for studying engagement in complex, naturalistic interactions.

Training-free motion statistics match self-supervised behavior encoders

Ziwen Chen • 2026-11-18

Self-supervised encoders for animal behavior are often trained on, or fed, hand-built movement features. We ask whether they beat those ingredients when compared fairly. A training-free representation, made of generic per-frame measurements summarized over fixed time windows and reduced with PCA, is compared with retrained and released encoders (BAMS, hBehaveMAE) under a preregistered protocol: frozen representations of equal size and one shared classifier. On CalMS21 it reaches 85.8 macro F1 against 82.4 for the best encoder, ties with four labelled videos, and leads when the annotator changes or new behaviors must be learned from a few videos; the reading holds for encoders retrained with three seeds. On the MABe22 mouse triplets it scores 83.7 against 80.3 for the released hBehaveMAE (gap +3.4, 95% interval +2.1 to +4.7). On a second species, the MABe22 fly groups, the same recipe scores 51.6 mean F1 over 50 tasks against 48.2 for BAMS, the best published method. On Shot7M2, the synthetic basketball benchmark built by hBehaveMAE's authors, it scores 68.8 against 64.7 for hBehaveMAE, the best method in that paper, though hBehaveMAE remains better on the shortest movements. Short time windows carry most of the result. Raw keypoints in each body's own frame, summarized over the same windows, already match or beat the encoders on both mouse datasets and on Shot7M2; on the fly groups they fall short, and the measurements add 5.3 points. On these benchmarks, current behavior encoders have yet to beat the hand-built features they learn from, and such baselines should be reported alongside them.

Inferring Neural Activity Changes under Recording Uncertainty with Transformers and Variational Inference

Nilson Chapagain, Pooja Chapagain • 2026-11-18

A difference in recorded firing rate across days may reflect a change in neuronal activity, missed spikes or recording a different neuron. We present a statistical approach that combines neuron tracking with Bayesian estimates of stationary mean recorded firing rates. Bounds under assumed differences in spike detection connect recorded rates to biological rate changes. We assess recording loss and uncertainty in rate changes in simulations, and count-model fit in recordings. We also compare two transformer estimators with Gaussian and flow variational inference under a shared temporal negative-binomial model. Flow variational inference agrees most closely with reference posteriors, while predictive performance varies across mice. Rate-change intervals contain true simulated changes less often than intended. In UnitMatch recordings from five mice, some inferred changes remain supported when spike detection is allowed to differ between days; others depend on equal detection. These interpretations require a correct neuron match. The approach identifies which inferred rate changes remain supported under specified recording assumptions and where uncertainty limits interpretation.

Characterizing adsorption thermodynamics in carbon capture materials: an efficient first-principles approach

Anna T. Bui, Grant M. Rotskoff, Thomas E. Markland • 2026-11-18

Porous materials offer a vast design space for CO$_2$ capture, whose exploration hinges on efficient and accurate first-principles predictions of the thermodynamics governing adsorption. However, simulating isotherms and heats of adsorption using grand canonical molecular simulations is extremely computationally expensive when combined with accurate ab initio treatments of the molecular interactions, limiting screening to low-pressure uptake encoded in the Henry constant. Identifying and designing the best CO$_2$ capture materials requires a strategy that can efficiently calculate their adsorption properties using ab initio potential energy surfaces such as those now available from foundational machine-learned interatomic potentials (MLIPs). Here we develop a neural classical density functional (cDFT) framework for carbon capture processes that allows direct calculation of adsorption isotherms from low to high loading limits of metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) without grand canonical simulations. We demonstrate the success of our approach by calculating the ab initio adsorption isotherms for over 5,000 experimentally reported MOFs. From our screening, we show how these materials can be characterized using four isotherm shapes, arising from their pore size and binding sites, which the Henry constant cannot distinguish. We show that these shapes, rather than the binding strength alone, decide which MOFs suit which process: small pores with strong binding sites for vacuum and temperature swings at low pressure, large pores with weakly binding walls for pressure swings. Finally, we identify the most promising candidates by weighing their working capacity against the energy needed for regeneration.

Variance-Optimal Counterfactual Policy Design for Sample-Efficient Online A/B Testing

Shashank Gupta, Subhadeep Maji • 2026-11-18

Online A/B testing is the standard approach for evaluating changes to user-facing systems, such as a new recommendation or ranking policy, before deployment. While the difference-in-means estimator of a standard A/B test is unbiased, it often suffers from high variance. When the treatment and production policies choose the same actions with similar probabilities, the resulting observations can add noise while providing little information about the difference between the policies. Consequently, A/B tests can require large amounts of traffic and long durations to detect small treatment effects. Existing variance-reduction methods mainly improve the estimator after the data-collection scheme has been fixed, and recent overlap-aware designs only optimize a single traffic split between the two policies; thereby, they cannot direct traffic to the actions on which the policies disagree. In this paper, we jointly optimize how actions are sampled and how the resulting observations are used to estimate the treatment effect. Our contributions are two-fold. First, building on pairwise off-policy evaluation, we derive the variance-optimal action-level logging policy for estimating the average treatment effect between two policies, which has a closed-form solution for any fixed control variate. Second, we use historical logs to jointly optimize the logging policy and a control variate to further reduce variance. Our experimental results in simulated policy A/B tests show that our designs estimate the average treatment effect more accurately than existing variance reduction methods on every evaluated setting, while generally improving statistical power and reducing how often the worse policy is selected. As a result, our designs enable practitioners to reach reliable deployment decisions with considerably less online traffic.

Sex Differences in the Detection of Parkinson's Disease from Speech

Hiba Akhaddar, Peter Plantinga, Mirco Ravanelli, Tristan Glatard • 2026-11-18

Parkinson's disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disease in the world, and 70\% to 75\% of PD patients suffer from speech disorders at some stage. Since PD affects men more frequently than women, previous publications tend to rely on unbalanced datasets that potentially introduce sex biases into trained models. This study investigates sex differences in PD detection from speech using deep learning and traditional machine learning approaches. We evaluated speech-based PD detection using embeddings from three pre-trained speech models, combined with handcrafted acoustic features and different classifiers. Sex-specific experiments were conducted on balanced subsets of four sustained phonation datasets, controlling for sex, disease status, and, where possible, age. All recordings were obtained during a sustained phonation task. Results show that female speakers were more accurately classified than male speakers in the larger dataset. Sex-specific results show that when the models are trained on one sex, they fail to generalize to the other one. Our results highlight the importance of including sex as a variable in the development of PD detection systems.
